# How the scores work

PrimeTime gives every finished event a score from 0 to 10 for **how exciting it was to watch**, not for who played well or who won. It never shows the result. This page explains where the numbers come from, sport by sport, and the rules that keep them spoiler-free.

Every score can be checked: under each match, **⚠ Why this score?** (behind a spoiler warning) lists every line that went into it.

## Football

Football is scored on **four pillars**. Each one gets its own score from 0 to 10, and the match score is their average (25% each). Then "the occasion" nudges it up or down.

### The four pillars

**Chances: how many good chances both teams created.** Scored or not, so a 0–0 full of near misses can still score well.
- Expected goals (xG): the quality of every shot. Nothing under 1.5 xG in total, full points at 4.0.
- Big chances, shots that tested the keeper (xG on target), and touches in the opposition box.
- Near misses: shots off the post or bar, goals ruled out by VAR, penalties.
- Without xG data, shots on target and the number of shots stand in. The card then says "limited info".

**Tempo: how the match flowed.**
- End to end: both teams attacking about equally, by shots, rather than one-way traffic.
- Directness: lots of shots and corners for the passes played, rather than slow possession.

**Tension: how long the result was in the balance.**
- How much of the match (stoppage time included) was within one goal.
- Equalisers, the lead changing hands, and comebacks from two goals down.
- **Suspense:** using the pre-match odds, the score and the time left, PrimeTime works out minute by minute how open the result still was. A match that stayed open to the end scores; one settled early doesn't.
- **Swing:** how much the goals moved the win chances. A goal that turns a match counts more than a fourth goal in a rout.
- Against the run of play: the team that created less won.

**Drama: what actually happened.**
- Goals (the first four count most), and great goals from outside the box or from unlikely chances.
- Late drama: goals from 75' while the result was still open (more from 85', most for a late equaliser or winner), big chances missed late, extra time and penalty shootouts.
- Red cards (more when the team ahead is down to ten), and lots of yellow cards.
- Upsets: the underdog winning or drawing, sized by how big an underdog it was according to the odds.

Each pillar has a soft ceiling, so piling up more of the same counts less and less. A typical match scores about 6 in each pillar. No line is counted twice: a match decided early already scores low on Tension, so it isn't also taken off in Drama.

### The occasion

- **Big match:** two strong teams make a thriller worth more than the same match between two weak ones: up to ×1.15 for the strongest pairs, down to ×0.73 for the weakest. Strength comes from Elo ratings (Club Elo for clubs, World Football Elo for national teams). A club counts half against its own league and half on a worldwide scale, so the best of Eliteserien is a strong team, but a top Premier League pair still counts for more.
- **What was at stake:** a title race (+0.5), a relegation battle (+0.4), or a race for the top 4, Europe, promotion, the play-offs or qualifying (+0.2), from the table before kick-off.
- **The top end:** above 7.7, each extra point counts a little less, so a 10 is almost impossible. A 9 really means something.

### Skip tips and the heat strip (football)

- The **heat strip** has six blocks of 15 minutes each, coloured by how much happened in them: goals, chances, cards, VAR and so on. Extra time is folded into the last block, so the strip can't reveal that there was any.
- **Skip tips** come from quiet 5-minute spells, roughly no more than one shot on target, like "Skip 20'–50'" or "Start at 35'". There are at most three. They stop a little before the action, so you get the build-up.
- The better the match, the longer a quiet spell must be before it's worth skipping. A 9 or more is always "Watch it all", and under 3 is "Highlights".

## Tennis (Grand Slam singles, ATP Masters 1000 and the ATP Finals)

- **The distance:** the full five (or three) sets gets the most; four sets in a best-of-five gets some.
- **Close sets:** a tiebreak or 7–5 counts fully, 6–4 a bit, 6–1 hardly at all. Each tiebreak adds more, and a final-set tiebreak most.
- **Comebacks:** the winner lost the first set, or the first two.
- **The round:** quarterfinals, semifinals and the final add a little.
- A retirement scores 1. Walkovers and qualifying are left out.

There's no heat strip for tennis, because its length would show how many sets were played. Skip tips only ever name set 1 (or sets 1–2 in best-of-five), because those are always played and never the deciding set.

## Formula 1

- **Overtakes:** only clean, on-track passes count. Passes on lap 1, passes during pit stops and passes undone within two laps are filtered out. Passes for the top 5, and especially the podium places, count extra.
- **Safety cars, virtual safety cars and red flags**, retirements and rain.
- **A close finish:** the winning margin (under 1 s, 1–3 s, 3–10 s), and a fight at the front in the last 20% of the race.
- **Sprints** are about a third of a race, so their overtakes count three times as much.
- **Qualifying isn't scored at all.** Any score for qualifying gave too much away about pole position.

The score never shows whether the driver on pole won: a pass for the lead counts the same as a pass for 2nd or 3rd. The heat strip always has ten equal slices. Skip tips always keep the first laps and the last 15% of the race, and there are none after a red flag, so a shortened race can't be spotted.

## Winter sports

- **Biathlon:** how close the finish was (under 1 s is a photo finish) and how close third place was. Also lead changes after each shooting, the last shooting turning the race, comebacks in pursuits and mass starts, and chaos at the range.
- **Alpine skiing:** the winning margin (under 0.05 s gets the most), a tight top 5, and in two-run races a comeback from 4th or worse after run 1. In one-run races, a skier starting 30th or later reaching the podium.
- **Cross-country skiing:** the winning margin, a big group at the finish, and a close podium.

**Which part to watch:** races where skiers start one by one say which bibs (start numbers) to watch, like "Watch bibs 1–10 and 41–50": the windows of ten where the top 3 started. That's alpine downhill and super-G, biathlon sprints and individuals, and cross-country interval starts. Slalom and giant slalom say it per run, like "Run 1: bibs 1–10, then the last 15 in run 2". Run 2 starts in reverse order, so the leader after run 1 goes last. These tips give a small hint (a late window means someone from far back did well), which is accepted for being more useful. Head-to-head races (mass starts, pursuits, relays) keep "Watch it all" or a shooting/leg to start from.

Athlete names are never shown on winter cards, because start lists give earlier results away.

## Never a spoiler

The rules above all serve one idea: **nothing on a card may tell you how it ended**.
- Results, scores and winners never reach the page's main data. They're kept in separate files that are only downloaded after you accept a warning.
- An automatic test checks every update. It fails on anything that looks like a score ("2–1"), on words like "winner", "comeback" or "late", and on any field that isn't allowed.
- Heat strips have fixed lengths, so they don't show extra time, a short race or the number of sets.
- Skip tips never cover the last 15 minutes of a football match, the deciding set or the end of a race.
- Team names can be hidden too, and tennis names are hidden by default.

## Make it your own

Under **Filters and settings → Custom scoring (football)** you can weigh the four pillars yourself, for example more Drama and less Tempo. Under "Advanced" you can tune every part. Your scores are worked out on your own device; everyone else keeps seeing the standard ones.

## How it was tested

The scores were checked against well-known matches: thrillers have to score above dull games, upsets above the same result the expected way round, and so on. Each change to the formula was back-tested on the last month of real matches, roughly 150, to keep the overall level steady. The "Excitement so far" number on live football was checked the same way: by the final whistle it's usually within about one point of the real score.

The data is free and public: ESPN (match events, odds, line-ups), FotMob (xG and match stats), OpenF1, IBU (biathlon) and FIS (alpine and cross-country), plus the Elo ratings. All the weights are in [`src/scoring/weights.mjs`](src/scoring/weights.mjs), each with a plain-language note.
